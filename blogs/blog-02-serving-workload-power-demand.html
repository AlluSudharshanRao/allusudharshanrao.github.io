From requests to facility load

The last post separated prefill, decode, and idle on a single GPU. This one steps back: a user request is also an electrical event once you have enough traffic. Software choices at the scheduler show up on the rack PDU and, after overhead, at the utility meter.

Active requests beat raw RPS

Requests per second (RPS) is easy to chart. It is also incomplete. What loads the machine is how many requests are in flight at once: past prefill, still holding KV cache, still decoding. Call that count At at time t.

Two services at 100 RPS can have very different At. Short prompts and short answers turn over quickly; long agentic jobs sit in decode for tens of seconds. Same arrival rate, very different occupancy, memory pressure, and watts.

The derivative matters too. A sharp rise in At means many new prompts hit prefill together. That is when you see short, correlated power spikes. A sharp drop means many completions at once and a quick dip before the next wave.

Example. Suppose average request lifetime is 20 seconds and RPS is 50. Rough steady-state occupancy is about 50 × 20 = 1000 active requests fleet-wide. Double output length without changing RPS and you roughly double At and the time spent in decode-heavy power—not because arrivals increased, but because work stays open longer.

The signal chain

Think of serving as a chain of transformations, not a single multiplier from “users” to “megawatts”:

Fixing one link does not fix the rest. A smooth arrival process can still produce spiky power if prefill aligns. Efficient GPUs can still produce swinging rack load if every server shares the same batch rhythm.

What shapes the electrical signature

FactorEffect on power
Prompt lengthLonger prefill, higher peak watts, superlinear cost with context
Output lengthLonger decode plateaus; dominates At for chatty workloads
Batching policyHigher utilization, more mixed phases, harder to predict from averages
Latency SLOsStrict TTFT favors prefill; changes queueing and phase mix
Model typeDense vs MoE changes per-token work and variance
Traffic burstsViral spikes, cron jobs, and quota resets align prefill across the fleet

Scale: from GPU to campus

Order-of-magnitude numbers help intuition (your hardware will differ):

LevelIllustrative IT load
One H100-class GPU at high load~700 W
8-GPU inference server~6–10 kW
Rack of several such servers~80–120 kW
Row of racks~1–2 MW
Large AI site10s–100s MW IT before PUE

At each step, swings below either add (if synchronized) or partially cancel (if independent). Inference fleets often sit in the middle: not as lockstep as training, but correlated enough that traffic spikes matter.

PUE: IT watts are not billable watts

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT equipment power. A site at 100 MW IT and PUE 1.3 presents about 130 MW to the grid. Planning from GPU averages alone systematically understates what utilities and breakers see.

PUE is not a constant. It moves with ambient temperature, cooling mode, and how fully loaded the IT is. A lightly loaded hall can look worse on PUE than a busy one because fixed cooling overhead is spread over fewer useful watts. That nonlinearity is why facility models need more than a single multiplier.

The point of common coupling (PCC) is where the datacenter meets the grid. Interconnection studies, tariffs, and ride-through design care about PCC demand, not dashboard GPU utilization.

What to measure (before you model)

The next post builds traces from workload features. In practice you anchor that with a few measurements:

  • GPU power. NVML or DCGM at 1–10 Hz (per GPU). Good for phase structure; know your driver sampling limits.
  • Server inlet / PDU. Rack or node meters at similar or lower rate. Captures host, NIC, and multi-GPU sharing.
  • Scheduler metrics. Active requests, queue depth, batch size, prefill vs decode time—aligned in time with power samples.
  • Facility meters. Slower, but ground truth for aggregation and PUE checks.

Align timestamps across layers. A spike in At that lines up with a prefill-heavy interval on the power trace is worth more than either signal alone.

Correlation across servers

If each server’s power wobbles independently, fleet variance often shrinks with size. If they move together—same traffic spike, same model, same batching cadence—peaks add. Serving is usually between those extremes: partial correlation during viral or scheduled events, more independence across heterogeneous pools.

That is the bridge to modeling: you need traffic-shaped features (At, ΔAt, occupancy, queue) and you need to know whether your fleet amplifies or damps them when you roll up to the PCC.
