When AI datacenters and the grid share a control loop

Based on the OpenG2G paper [1]. Builds on datacenter power measurement: we measured how AI loads wiggle; here we ask what operators can do about it while serving still runs.

AI datacenters are no longer background loads on the grid. A single modern site can draw tens of megawatts; planned campuses are sized in gigawatts—on the order of 1.7× the average load of San Francisco for one 1 GW site [1]. That scale slows interconnection, pushes new transmission, and makes voltage and reliability studies harder. One response gaining traction is runtime power flexibility: the datacenter agrees to raise or lower draw on short notice by turning software knobs, instead of waiting years for new wires.

Runtime coordination, not siting studies

OpenG2G focuses on AI datacenter–grid runtime coordination [1]: a closed loop where grid state (voltages, violations) and datacenter state (power, throughput, latency) co-evolve, and a controller issues commands to either side at millisecond-to-minute cadence. That is different from interconnection planning or picking a site on a map. The question here is: given a running LLM fleet, how much can you move power without breaking SLOs, and what does that do to the feeder?

Inference is the natural workload to study first: industry estimates put 80–90% of AI compute demand on inference, not training [1,20,38,39].

Grid picture for ML engineers

Consumers attach at buses linked by lines. Bulk power enters at a source bus (substation or generator). A datacenter is modeled as one large load whose watts follow whatever the GPUs are doing.

Operators keep bus voltages in a tight band (often within about ±5% of nominal). Undervoltage stresses equipment and stability; overvoltage is also out of limits. Classic grid actuators—tap changers, capacitor banks, storage—have latencies from sub-second to minutes. A datacenter that can shed or absorb megawatts in seconds gives the operator another lever while mechanical devices catch up.

When the grid is stressed vs when it has headroom

Grid conditionTypical signalDatacenter response
Undervoltage / high net loadVoltage below bandShed load: smaller batches, fewer replicas, lower power
Overvoltage / excess generationVoltage above bandAbsorb load: larger batches within latency limits
NormalVoltage in bandRun for throughput; keep flexibility in reserve

The knobs are the same ones you already tune for serving—model, precision, parallelism, batch size, replica count—only now they answer to grid metrics as well as tok/s and latency [7,8].

How OpenG2G scores coordination

A headline metric in the paper is integral voltage violation (pu·s): the time integral of how far bus voltages sit outside the allowed ±5% band. It rewards fixes that are both short and shallow. Token throughput (tok/s) captures the AI side. Good coordination improves violation without destroying serving economics.

Bridge from power measurement. Your FFT and ramp analysis tells you which frequencies the load excites. Integral violation tells you whether the grid stayed within voltage limits while you moved that load. Both belong in the same story.
